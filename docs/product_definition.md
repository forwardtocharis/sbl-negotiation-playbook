# Product Definition

Synthetic demonstrator, lender-side (private bank). Not legal advice.

## SBL vs securities lending

A securities-based loan is credit extended to a client secured against their existing investment portfolio, where the securities themselves stay pledged (not sold or transferred out) and the client continues to own the economic upside and downside of the portfolio; this is structurally and regulatorily distinct from securities lending, which is the temporary transfer of securities themselves — typically to a short seller or market-maker, under a Global Master Securities Lending Agreement — in exchange for a fee, and has nothing to do with extending credit against a client's own wealth.

## Lombard lending terminology

Swiss and continental European private banking calls this product a **Lombard loan**, after the medieval Lombard merchants who lent against pledged goods; UK and US private banking and legal practice more commonly say **securities-based lending (SBL)**, **securities-backed lending**, or, in a US idiom, a **margin loan**. All four terms describe the same underlying structure: cash advanced against a pledge of a securities portfolio, with the advance capped by a lending value (advance rate / LTV) set per asset class. An interviewer working across EMEA private banking is as likely to use "Lombard" as "SBL," and the two should be used interchangeably once the definition above is established, rather than treated as different products.

## The three-document set

**Facility letter.** This is the commercial and credit document: it states the facility type (uncommitted demand or committed term), amount, currency, benchmark rate, conditions precedent, eligible collateral and lending values, concentration limits, events of default, representations (including capacity, no-advice/non-reliance, and purpose), and governing law. It is signed by the lender and the borrower (and, if a corporate or trust structure, whoever has authority to bind that entity — a director, trustee, or authorised signatory). It is the document a relationship manager negotiates line by line with the client or the client's counsel, and it is the anchor from which the other two documents are cross-referenced.

**Security agreement (pledge / charge / title-transfer agreement).** This is the document that actually creates the lender's proprietary interest in the collateral — either a pledge or charge (security interest, title stays with the pledgor) or a title-transfer collateral arrangement (full ownership passes to the lender subject to a return obligation). It sets out margin maintenance mechanics, the margin call and cure period, the security's scope over eligible collateral, and the lender's enforcement rights on default. It is signed by the pledgor, who is the borrower in most retail-style Lombard deals but can be a third party (spouse, trust, holding company) in a meaningful minority of private-banking deals, and by the lender as secured party.

**Account control agreement.** Needed only where the lender is not itself the custodian of the pledged assets. It is a tripartite agreement between the pledgor, the lender (as secured party), and the third-party custodian, under which the custodian agrees to act on the lender's instructions regarding the pledged account without needing the pledgor's further cooperation. This is the document that operationally perfects control over intermediated securities held outside the lending bank, and its absence (or a custodian's refusal to sign one on the lender's preferred terms) is one of the most common practical blockers to closing an SBL facility with an external custodian.

## Negotiation dynamic in private wealth management

SBL negotiations in a private bank sit at the intersection of two structurally opposed pressures: the relationship manager wants the facility documented and drawn quickly, on terms the client will accept, because the loan deepens the client relationship and the RM is measured on assets and revenue, not on the fallback ladder; credit and legal are measured on the bank not carrying risk it cannot price, perfect, or enforce, and are structurally the only voice in the room arguing for slower, more protective drafting. Left unmanaged, this produces two failure modes: ad hoc concessions that erode the bank's protection deal by deal with no institutional memory of why, and senior lawyer time consumed re-deriving the same fallback position on every deal. A tiered playbook with fixed escalation points addresses both: routine concessions (the "acceptable" tier) can be agreed by the deal lawyer without going back to a senior colleague, protection-costing concessions (the "fallback" tier) are conditioned and require a named approver every time, and walk-away positions ("unacceptable") are hard-coded so a junior lawyer under relationship-manager pressure has a fixed line to point to rather than a judgement call to make alone under time pressure.

## What this demonstrator is and is not

This is a demonstrator built from public sources — legislation, secondary commentary, and a small number of filed exhibits reviewed for structure, not wording — to show how a negotiation playbook for SBL/Lombard facilities can be made structured, auditable, and partly automated. It is not a validated market position, not legal advice, and not something any institution should run in production. Every substantive drafting position in this repository carries a `status` of `stub` or `draft`; none has been promoted to `reviewed`. The rules engine and jurisdiction overlay make explicit where a conclusion has not been checked against primary law (`[VERIFY]`) and list, in the not-automated register, every decision this tool refuses to make on its own. Before any of this could be used inside a real institution it would need validation against that institution's own credit policy and risk appetite, and against local counsel in each governing-law jurisdiction.
